\chapter{Experimental Evaluation}
\label{cha:evaluation}

This chapter reports the results of comparing nine RAG architectures on two
benchmarks: HotpotQA, a large encyclopedic multi-hop dataset, and a purpose-built
Drug--Drug Interaction (DDI) benchmark in the target medical domain.
Eight metrics are evaluated: three overlap-based accuracy measures, four
RAGAS-based RAG-quality indicators, and wall-clock query latency.

The central question is whether the additional machinery of the more elaborate
architectures --- query transformation, agentic decomposition, debate, set-wise
evidence selection, grounding verification --- buys anything over a plain
retrieve-then-generate pass, and if so, where.
The short answer, developed over the rest of this chapter, is that it depends
almost entirely on how hard the questions are: the same architecture that hurts
on HotpotQA helps on the hardest DDI questions.

\section{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:exp_setup}

\subsection{Benchmark Datasets}

\paragraph{HotpotQA.}
HotpotQA~\cite{yang2018hotpotqa} is a multi-hop question answering dataset
built from Wikipedia.
Each question requires reasoning over two or more documents to produce a short
factoid answer; the dataset was chosen specifically to stress the retrieval layer,
since a system that retrieves only one relevant document typically cannot answer
correctly.
For this evaluation, 1{,}000 questions were drawn uniformly at random from the
7{,}405-question distractor-setting validation split --- large enough to yield
stable metric estimates while remaining computationally tractable across nine
RAG configurations.

Two representative questions illustrate the multi-hop structure:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textit{``What government position was held by the woman who portrayed
    Corliss Archer in the film \emph{Kiss and Tell}?''}\\
    \textbf{Gold:} \textit{``Chief of Protocol''} ---
    requires a two-step chain: retrieve the cast of \emph{Kiss and Tell} (1945)
    to identify Shirley Temple, then retrieve Temple's later career to find her
    role as United States Chief of Protocol under President Ford.
    No single Wikipedia article answers both hops.

  \item \textit{``The winner of the 1954 Nobel Prize in Literature was born
    in which US state?''}\\
    \textbf{Gold:} \textit{``Illinois''} ---
    requires identifying the 1954 laureate (Ernest Hemingway) from the Nobel
    Prize article, then retrieving Hemingway's biography to confirm his
    birthplace in Oak Park, Illinois.
    Both documents are required; neither alone is sufficient.
\end{itemize}

HotpotQA is not a drug-interaction benchmark.
It was chosen as a neutral, widely-used multi-hop reasoning benchmark that
stresses retrieval without requiring domain-specific knowledge, and it serves
here as the high-power arm of the evaluation: with 1{,}000 questions per mode,
differences of a few points are statistically resolvable.

\paragraph{DDI benchmark.}
The second benchmark targets the actual application domain and is described in
detail in \cref{subsec:ddi_dataset}: 100 drug-interaction questions, 20 at each
of five difficulty levels, built from OpenFDA drug labelling and DrugBank
interaction records.
It is far smaller than HotpotQA, and therefore far weaker statistically, but it
is the benchmark that actually resembles the workload the system is designed for.
The contrast between the two datasets turns out to carry most of this chapter's
findings.

\subsection{Evaluation Metrics}

Eight metrics are reported, grouped by what they measure.

\paragraph{Overlap-based accuracy.}
\emph{Token F1} measures the token-level overlap between the predicted and gold
answer after lowercasing and punctuation removal, and is the primary accuracy
metric on HotpotQA.
\emph{Exact Match (EM)} is stricter: it assigns 1 only when the prediction matches
the gold answer exactly after normalisation, and 0 otherwise.
\emph{ROUGE-L} uses the longest common subsequence between prediction and gold
and is reported for completeness; on short factoid answers it behaves similarly
to Token~F1.

\paragraph{RAGAS quality metrics.}
\emph{Faithfulness} measures the fraction of atomic claims in the generated
answer that are supported by the retrieved context, as assessed by an LLM
judge~\cite{es2023ragas}.
A score of 1.0 indicates that every claim in the answer is grounded in the
retrieved passages; a score below 1.0 indicates at least some hallucination.
\emph{Answer Correctness} measures agreement with the gold answer on the level of
meaning rather than string overlap, and is the primary accuracy metric on the DDI
benchmark, where clinical explanations are semantically correct but lexically
nothing like the reference text.
\emph{Answer Relevance} is estimated by generating reverse questions from the
answer and measuring the cosine similarity of their embeddings to the original
query; it captures whether the answer is on-topic rather than merely factually
present.
\emph{Context Recall} measures the fraction of the gold answer that can be
attributed to the retrieved context, indicating whether the retrieval stage
surfaced all necessary evidence.

\paragraph{Latency.}
Wall-clock latency per query is measured as the mean and 95th-percentile
of per-query durations including all pipeline stages and LLM calls.

\subsection{Statistical Method}
\label{subsec:stat_method}

Two of the reported metrics --- faithfulness and answer correctness --- are
produced by an LLM judge, whose scores carry noise, and all metrics are averaged
over question samples that are themselves finite.
Reporting mode-level means alone would therefore invite over-reading of
differences that are indistinguishable from sampling error.

Every comparison in this chapter is instead expressed as a \emph{paired contrast
against the Vanilla control}, computed question by question within a benchmark.
For each mode $M$ and each question $q$, the quantity of interest is
$\Delta_q = \text{score}_M(q) - \text{score}_{\text{Vanilla}}(q)$.
Because both modes answer the same question under the same retrieval backend and
the same judge, question difficulty and any quirk of the judge affect both terms
and largely cancel in the difference.
Significance is assessed with a Wilcoxon signed-rank test over the paired scores.
This is a non-parametric test: rather than assuming the differences follow a normal
distribution, it ranks the paired differences by magnitude and asks whether the
positive and negative ranks are balanced, which makes it appropriate for bounded,
non-normally distributed metrics such as these.
The mean of $\Delta$ is reported with a bootstrap 95\,\% confidence interval
(10{,}000 resamples): the paired differences are resampled with replacement
10{,}000 times, the mean is recomputed for each resample, and the interval spans
the 2.5th to 97.5th percentiles of that distribution of means --- an interval that
contains the true mean advantage with 95\,\% confidence and requires no
distributional assumption.
Throughout, \sig{***} denotes $p < 0.001$, \sig{**} denotes $p < 0.01$, and
\sig{*} denotes $p < 0.05$; \emph{n.s.} (not significant) marks a difference at
$p \geq 0.05$, i.e.\ one that cannot be distinguished from sampling noise.

The full analysis is reproducible from \texttt{scripts/thesis\_final\_analysis.py},
which reads per-question metrics from the \texttt{eval\_results} collection in
MongoDB and regenerates every number in this chapter.

\subsection{Systems Under Evaluation}

Nine RAG configurations were evaluated on the same \medrag{} platform with only
the \texttt{rag\_mode} setting changed per run.
\cref{tab:mode_descriptions} summarises each mode.
All modes share the same retrieval backend (Weaviate~1.27, hybrid search
$\alpha = 0.5$, top-$k = 20$ before reranking, top-$5$ after cross-encoder
reranking), the same embedding model (OpenAI \texttt{text-embedding-3-large},
served through OpenRouter), and the same generator LLM (\texttt{gpt-5-nano}, also
through OpenRouter).
No special prompts or chain-of-thought instructions were added beyond what each
architecture requires.

\paragraph{Fidelity to the source methods.}
Four of the nine modes are prompt-based approximations rather than complete
re-implementations of the papers they are named after, and since this chapter
compares them against each other, the gaps have to be on the record.

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Self-Reflection} approximates Self-RAG~\cite{asai2024selfrag}.
    The original emits per-segment reflection tokens from a fine-tuned decoder;
    this implementation scores the whole drafted answer with an ordinary LLM call
    and re-drafts once if the score is below 0.7.
  \item \textbf{Corrective RAG} approximates CRAG~\cite{yan2024crag} and omits its
    two defining components.
    There is no fine-tuned retrieval evaluator --- relevance is judged by
    thresholding the raw hybrid retrieval score at 0.3 --- and, more importantly,
    there is no web-search fallback.
    When the corrective step fires, this implementation re-queries \emph{the same
    corpus} with a broadened search (lower $\alpha$, doubled top-$k$), whereas
    CRAG's central claim is that it can recover from corpus gaps by reaching
    outside the corpus entirely.
    A closed-corpus CRAG cannot express that claim, and its results here should be
    read as measuring a relevance-gated re-retrieval strategy, not CRAG proper.
  \item \textbf{Query Rewriting} follows the Rewrite--Retrieve--Read
    pattern~\cite{ma2023rewrite} but uses a prompted rewriter, not the small
    reinforcement-learning-trained rewriter of the original.
  \item \textbf{HyDE} concatenates the hypothetical document with the original
    query before embedding, rather than embedding the hypothetical document
    alone~\cite{gao2022hyde}; in preliminary testing the concatenation was more
    robust when the generated document drifted off-topic.
\end{itemize}

This caveat is worth keeping in view because it cuts against the chapter's own
recommendations: Corrective RAG is recommended in \cref{subsec:when_rarerag} as
the safest default, and Query Rewriting is the best buy on hard clinical questions,
and \emph{both} are approximated modes.
Whether a faithful CRAG with a web-search fallback would do better still is untested
here, and it is a live possibility rather than a rhetorical hedge: a corpus-gap
recovery mechanism is exactly what the DDI benchmark's hardest questions would
reward.

\subsection{Hardware and Software Environment}
\label{subsec:hw_env}

All experiments were run on a single Apple MacBook Pro with an Apple M1 Pro
system-on-chip and 16\,GB of unified memory.
No discrete GPU was available.
The one component that ran locally was the cross-encoder reranker
(BGE-reranker-v2-m3), which executed on the integrated GPU via Metal, as Apple
Silicon is not supported by CUDA or ROCm.
Both the embedding model (OpenAI \texttt{text-embedding-3-large}) and the generator
LLM (\texttt{gpt-5-nano}) were served through OpenRouter and therefore executed on
remote infrastructure; the local machine's role for those two components was only
to issue API calls and collect results.
Although the platform also supports a fully local embedding model (BGE-M3 via
\texttt{sentence-transformers}), the reported experiments used the API-served
model.

The software stack ran inside Docker Compose: Weaviate~1.27 (vector store),
MongoDB~7 (document and conversation storage), and RabbitMQ~3.13 (message
broker) each in a dedicated container, with all \medrag{} microservices built
on Python~3.12 and managed with \texttt{uv}.

\paragraph{The LLM judge is not the generator.}
Faithfulness, Answer Correctness and Answer Relevance are scored by
\texttt{gpt-oss-120b} --- a 120-billion-parameter model, and thus a different and
considerably larger one than the \texttt{gpt-5-nano} used for generation.
When the judge and the generator are the same model, the judge tends to reward
answers that match its own parametric knowledge and phrasing --- a
self-preference bias that inflates scores for the very system under
test~\cite{wataoka2024llmjudge}.
Pinning the judge to a separate model removes that particular failure mode.
The same judge scored every mode in both benchmarks, so judge behaviour is a
constant across all comparisons reported here.

\paragraph{Load conditions.}
Every benchmark run dispatched queries through a Python runner with two requests
in flight concurrently.
Concurrent queries contend for the local cross-encoder reranker, which inflates
absolute latency relative to an idle system, but it does so identically for all
nine modes, so the latency figures are comparable with each other.
They should not be compared against latency reported elsewhere in the literature
for the same architectures.

\label{sec:results_quality}

\cref{tab:hotpot_advantage} gives the paired contrast against Vanilla that the
discussion relies on.

Before turning to the differences between modes, it is worth stating what the
absolute numbers say about the platform itself, because they are strong.
On HotpotQA every mode retrieves nearly all of the evidence each question needs
--- context recall ranges from 0.913 to 0.986, and five of the nine modes exceed
0.96 --- which confirms that the hybrid retrieval and cross-encoder reranking
stages do their job well.
Grounding is correspondingly high: the best modes reach 0.973 faithfulness, and
even the plain Vanilla baseline is faithful at 0.939, meaning that on a
well-indexed corpus the system hallucinates in fewer than one answer in fifteen.
The differences examined below are therefore movements at the top of the scale,
on a system that already retrieves and grounds well; they are not a search for a
mode that merely works.

\paragraph{Accuracy is architecture-insensitive; the LLM is the bottleneck.}
Among the five lightweight modes, Token~F1 spans 0.539 to 0.547 --- a range of
0.008 across architectures that differ substantially in how they retrieve.
None of the four lightweight modes moves answer correctness away from Vanilla by
a statistically detectable margin (all $p > 0.15$).
Whatever limits accuracy on HotpotQA, it is not the retrieval strategy: it is the
capacity of a small generator model to extract the right short span from evidence
it has already been given.

\paragraph{Lightweight query transformation buys grounding, and it is real.}
HyDE, Query Rewriting, Self-Reflection and Corrective RAG each improve
faithfulness over Vanilla by roughly three points, and every one of those gains is
significant at $p < 0.001$ on 1{,}000 paired questions.
The effect is small but consistent and mechanistically sensible: all four modes
give the retriever a second, better-formed shot at the corpus --- an expanded
query, a hypothetical document, a validation-and-retry step --- and answers
generated from better evidence have less need to improvise.
Vanilla's 0.939 already leaves little headroom, which is precisely why a
three-point gain matters: it closes about half of the remaining gap.

\paragraph{The agentic modes are actively harmful here.}
All four agentic architectures land \emph{below} Vanilla on faithfulness, and
three of the four also lose on correctness, with MADAM-RAG shedding 12 points of
answer correctness ($p < 0.001$).
This is the single most counterintuitive result in this work, and it is not
noise: it rests on roughly 1{,}000 paired questions with $p < 0.001$.

The explanation is that every additional LLM call is another opportunity to drift
from the sources.
A HotpotQA question needs one or two Wikipedia passages and a short span lifted
out of them.
A planner that decomposes such a question into sub-tasks, executors that
summarise passages into ``findings'', and a synthesiser that writes the final
answer from those findings, has passed the evidence through three layers of
paraphrase before anything is committed to the answer --- and paraphrase is where
grounding leaks away.
MADAM-RAG compounds this: two agents write competing answers and a judge
reconciles them, which on a question with exactly one correct short answer
manufactures a disagreement that did not exist and resolves it into something
vaguer than either agent started with.
Context recall tells the same story from the retrieval side: MADAM-RAG (0.920) and
\rarerag{} (0.913) retrieve the least of the needed evidence, while Iterative
Multi-hop --- whose sequential chain is genuinely aimed at multi-hop questions ---
retrieves the most (0.986) and is the only agentic mode that holds its correctness
level with Vanilla ($+0.002$, n.s.).

\rarerag{}'s position deserves a direct statement, since it is the architecture
this thesis proposes.
On HotpotQA it is the \emph{worst} of the nine on faithfulness ($-0.054$ vs
Vanilla, $p < 0.001$).
The culprit is set-wise selection.
Instead of passing the top reranked passages straight to the generator, \rarerag{}
keeps only three passages chosen to be maximally \emph{complementary} --- it
deliberately favours passages that cover different ground and drops ones that
overlap.
That is a sensible strategy only when the evidence a question needs is spread
across many passages, so that diversity buys coverage.
A HotpotQA question is the opposite case: its answer usually sits in just two short
passages, and pruning for diversity discards one of exactly the two the generator
needed.
The mechanism that is supposed to help is the mechanism that hurts, because
HotpotQA violates the assumption it is built on --- that relevant evidence is
scattered rather than concentrated.

\section{Drug Interaction Benchmark Results}
\label{sec:ddi_eval}

\subsection{Dataset and Evaluation Protocol}
\label{subsec:ddi_dataset}

To evaluate all nine RAG architectures on the target medical domain, a
dedicated five-level Drug--Drug Interaction (DDI) benchmark was constructed
from two open-access corpora: FDA drug labelling data obtained via the
OpenFDA API and DrugBank interaction records from the Therapeutic Data Commons
(TDC) corpus~\cite{huang2021tdc}.
The retrieval corpus indexed for this benchmark comprises 4{,}688 documents ---
652 single-drug profile documents and 4{,}036 unique drug--drug interaction
records --- each ingested through the full pipeline (parse, fixed-size chunking,
embedding, and vector indexing in Weaviate) that
\cref{cha:implementation} describes.
The benchmark itself contains 100 question--answer pairs (20 per level),
stratified by reasoning complexity:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{L1 --- Single drug profile.}
    Questions about a single drug's indications, mechanism, or contraindications.
  \item \textbf{L2 --- Two-drug interaction.}
    Questions requiring retrieval of both drug profiles and an interaction record.
  \item \textbf{L3 --- Multi-hop reasoning.}
    Questions that require chaining evidence across three or more documents.
  \item \textbf{L4 --- Polypharmacy.}
    Questions involving four or more concurrent drugs and their combined risks.
  \item \textbf{L5 --- Expert PK/PD.}
    Questions requiring pharmacokinetic or pharmacodynamic reasoning
    (e.g.\ CYP enzyme inhibition, protein binding displacement).
\end{itemize}

\cref{tab:ddi_examples} gives one representative question--answer pair per level
to make the difficulty gradient concrete.

Each question was generated by prompting gpt-5-nano to produce a clinically
plausible scenario from the source documents, with the gold answer derived from
the same passages under a mandatory citation pattern that ties every claim back to
a specific source file.
All 100 question--answer pairs were then manually reviewed against their source
passages, and each was checked for factual consistency with the cited documents
before being admitted to the benchmark.
Token~F1 and Exact Match are reported but carry little meaning here: open-ended
clinical explanations never match a gold string exactly, which is why answer
correctness is the accuracy metric of record on this benchmark.
Every mode processed the same 100 questions.

\paragraph{The sign flips.}
The four agentic architectures that were significantly \emph{worse} than Vanilla
on HotpotQA are, on DDI, all at or above it on faithfulness, and Multi-Agent is
the best of the nine ($+0.108$, $p = 0.012$).
Query Rewriting gains a comparable amount ($+0.101$, $p = 0.009$).
Nothing about the architectures changed between the two benchmarks --- the same
code, the same retrieval backend, the same generator and judge answered both.
What changed is the questions.

The reason the direction reverses is visible in the Vanilla baseline itself.
On HotpotQA, Vanilla is already faithful at 0.939 and there is almost nothing to
add; the only thing extra reasoning can do is get in the way.
On DDI, Vanilla sits at 0.694, hallucinating on roughly one claim in three, and
the questions genuinely demand what the elaborate pipelines provide: several drug
profiles plus an interaction record, assembled into a mechanism.
When a task actually requires reasoning over multiple pieces of evidence, machinery
that decomposes and reassembles evidence begins to earn its cost.

\paragraph{Grounding improves; correctness does not.}
This is the honest limit of the result, and it should not be glossed over.
Not one of the eight modes improves answer correctness over Vanilla by a
statistically detectable margin --- Query Rewriting's $+0.052$ and Multi-Agent's
$+0.037$ are directionally right but sit comfortably inside their confidence
intervals.
Meanwhile MADAM-RAG \emph{loses} 8 points of correctness ($p = 0.026$) while
gaining 8 points of faithfulness.

That combination is not a contradiction; it is a description of what debate does.
MADAM-RAG's judge, faced with two agents that have argued from different angles,
converges on the claims both agents can support from the retrieved text --- and
hedges on everything else.
The result reads as ``there is a potential interaction; consult a pharmacist''
where Vanilla would have committed to a mechanism.
Such an answer is better grounded and less useful.
For a clinical advisory tool, that trade is not obviously wrong --- a hedge is
safer than a confident fabrication --- but it must be named for what it is rather
than sold as an improvement.

\paragraph{The effects grow with difficulty.}
Multi-Agent's faithfulness advantage rises from $+0.108$ over all 100 questions to
$+0.188$ on the 40 hardest, and Query Rewriting's from $+0.101$ to $+0.178$ ---
both still significant despite the sample shrinking by 60\,\%.
An effect that survives a smaller sample by growing is behaving like a real effect
and not like noise: if these gains were sampling artefacts, cutting $n$ from 100
to 40 would have dissolved them.
The gains concentrate exactly where Vanilla is weakest, which is the pattern one
would predict if the extra reasoning is doing the work it was designed to do.

This is the thesis's core empirical claim, and it can now be stated in one
sentence: \emph{architectural complexity pays for itself in proportion to how much
reasoning the question actually requires.}
On easy questions it is dead weight that degrades grounding (HotpotQA, DDI L1).
On questions that demand assembling several drug profiles into a mechanism
(DDI L4--L5), it recovers grounding that a single pass loses.
An advisory system serving a mixed case-load therefore should not commit to one
pipeline for everything --- which is the argument for a router, and the reason
\rarerag{} is built around one.

\paragraph{Two cautions, stated plainly.}
First, 40 questions is a small sample, and a 20-question cell is smaller still;
the significant results above should be read as well-supported \emph{directional}
findings, not as settled effect sizes.
The per-level table is a map of where to look, not a measurement of how much.
Second, and more important: the effects that survive testing are all in
\emph{faithfulness}.
On the hard subset, no mode significantly improves answer correctness, while
MADAM-RAG ($-0.220$, $p = 0.002$) and \rarerag{} ($-0.161$, $p = 0.031$)
significantly damage it.
The defensible summary of this benchmark is that the elaborate architectures make
the system's answers better grounded in its sources on hard questions, but no more
likely to be right --- and two of them make it markedly less likely to be right.
Grounding is worth something on its own in a clinical setting, where an answer
traceable to a cited FDA label can be checked by a pharmacist and a fabricated one
cannot.
It is not, however, the same thing as being correct, and this evaluation does not
show that any architecture here made the system more correct.

\subsection{Where \rarerag{} Actually Stands}
\label{subsec:rare_verdict}

\rarerag{} is the architecture proposed by this thesis, and it is the one the
data treats most harshly.
It is the worst mode on HotpotQA faithfulness ($-0.054$, $p < 0.001$); on DDI it
is mid-table in aggregate ($+0.064$, n.s.); and on the hard L4--L5 subset --- the
questions it was designed for --- it is slightly \emph{below} Vanilla on
faithfulness ($-0.034$, n.s.) and significantly below on correctness ($-0.161$,
$p = 0.031$).
The per-level table shows why the aggregate looked benign: \rarerag{} scores well
at L1--L3 (0.95, 0.86, 0.865) and then falls off a cliff at L4 (0.595).
Its gains on easy questions were masking its losses on hard ones.

The most plausible mechanism is set-wise selection (\cref{cha:rare_rag}), which
prunes the reranked passages down to $m = 3$ maximally complementary ones.
On an L4 polypharmacy question involving four drugs, three passages cannot cover
four drug profiles plus their pairwise interactions: the selector is starving the
generator of evidence at exactly the moment it needs the most.
The very diversity objective that makes the selection look principled is what
breaks it. Set-wise selection is designed to fix redundancy --- passages that
repeat the same fact --- by keeping only ones that add something new. But on a
polypharmacy question the problem is never redundancy; it is that there simply is
not enough room in three passages to hold every drug the question asks about.
What limits the answer is \emph{coverage} (how many of the needed facts are
present at all), not \emph{complementarity} (how little the passages overlap), and
the selector optimises the wrong one.

This is a negative result about a proposed contribution, and it is reported as
one.
It also suggests an obvious repair --- making $m$ a function of the query's
complexity class, which the router already computes --- and that is taken up in
\cref{cha:conclusions}.

\section{What the Complexity Costs}
\label{sec:results_latency}

The quality results say nothing about price, and the price is steep.
\cref{fig:results_latency} plots mean latency for all nine modes on both
benchmarks.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figures/fig_latency.png}
  \caption[Mean query latency for all nine RAG modes on both benchmarks.]{%
    Mean latency per query. All modes were measured under identical load
    (two concurrent queries), so the figures are comparable with each other.}
  \label{fig:results_latency}
\end{figure}

The spread is an order of magnitude.
On HotpotQA, Vanilla answers in 9.1\,s and Corrective RAG in 11.8\,s, while
Iterative Multi-hop needs 83.7\,s --- nine times as long for an architecture that
is significantly \emph{worse} on faithfulness.
On DDI the pattern repeats at a higher baseline: Vanilla 31.3\,s against
\rarerag{}'s 129.0\,s.

Read together with the quality results, the cost table decides the practical
recommendation of this chapter.
On the hard DDI questions, Multi-Agent (72.4\,s) and Query Rewriting (52.7\,s) buy
statistically indistinguishable amounts of grounding --- $+0.188$ and $+0.178$
faithfulness respectively.
One of them does it with a planner, a set of executor agents and a synthesiser;
the other does it with a single extra LLM call before retrieval, in 27\,\% less
time.
When a technique that simple matches a full agentic pipeline on the metric that
matters, on the questions that matter, the agentic pipeline has to justify its
remaining cost --- and on this evidence it cannot.

Iterative Multi-hop is worth a separate note, because it is the slowest mode on
DDI (128\,s).
That is not a measurement artefact but intrinsic to the method: each hop must wait
for what the previous hop established, so the retrieval steps run in sequence
rather than in parallel.
That sequential dependency is the entire point of a chain-of-retrieval
architecture, and it is also what makes it expensive.
The honest summary is that the method costs what it costs --- and on these two
benchmarks it does not repay it.

\section{Hypothesis Evaluation}
\label{sec:hypothesis_eval}

The introduction posed three research questions (\cref{sec:problem}).
RQ1 --- which architecture gives the best quality/latency trade-off, and whether
that depends on question difficulty --- is answered across
\cref{sec:results_quality,sec:ddi_eval,sec:results_latency} and settled in
\cref{subsec:when_rarerag}: there is no single winner, and difficulty decides.
RQ3 --- whether the platform is a reliable, source-traceable demonstrator --- is
addressed by the demonstration in \cref{sec:ddi_demo}.
This section closes RQ2, the \rarerag{} claim, which was sharpened in
\cref{sec:rarerag_hypotheses} into two falsifiable hypotheses tested here.

\paragraph{H1 --- Faithfulness.}
\emph{\rarerag{} achieves higher faithfulness than all single-path RAG architectures.}
\textbf{Rejected.}
\rarerag{} is significantly \emph{below} Vanilla on HotpotQA ($-0.054$,
$p < 0.001$) and not significantly different from it on DDI ($+0.064$, $p = 0.14$).
On the hard DDI questions it was designed for, it is below Vanilla on both
metrics.
The result holds with set-wise selection active and with a judge model distinct
from the generator, so it cannot be attributed to an inactive component or to
self-preference bias in the judge.

\paragraph{H2 --- Competitive accuracy.}
\emph{\rarerag{} maintains competitive accuracy despite the additional pipeline stages.}
\textbf{Rejected.}
Token~F1 on HotpotQA is 0.485 against Vanilla's 0.539 ($-0.054$, $p < 0.001$), and
answer correctness on hard DDI questions is 16 points below Vanilla ($p = 0.031$).
The additional stages cost accuracy rather than preserving it.

\paragraph{What the data supports instead.}
The hypotheses as originally posed were about one architecture.
The finding that the evidence does support is about a relationship: the benefit of
architectural complexity scales with task difficulty, is measurable in grounding
rather than in correctness, and reverses sign on tasks a single pass already
handles well.
That is a weaker claim than ``\rarerag{} wins'', and a more useful one, because it
predicts \emph{when} to reach for a complex pipeline instead of asserting that one
should always be preferred.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{Faithfulness and Correctness Are Not the Same Objective}
\label{subsec:faithfulness_orthogonality}

Across both benchmarks the two headline metrics move independently, and in the
case of MADAM-RAG they move in opposite directions.
This is not an artefact of the evaluation setup; it follows from what the metrics
mean.
RAGAS faithfulness asks whether the generated text is supported by the
\emph{retrieved} context, not whether that context is right.
A system that retrieves a plausible but irrelevant FDA label and reasons carefully
within it scores well on faithfulness and badly on correctness.
A system that quietly draws on parametric knowledge the retrieval missed can be
correct and unfaithful at once.

For a medical advisory system, that distinction is the whole design question.
A faithful answer is auditable: every claim points at a passage a pharmacist can
open and check.
An unfaithful but correct answer is right by luck and cannot be verified by the
person relying on it.
Optimising for faithfulness --- as \rarerag{} attempts --- is therefore a defensible
choice even when it costs accuracy, provided the cost is stated rather than hidden.
The results here show the cost is real: MADAM-RAG buys 14 points of grounding on
hard questions with 22 points of correctness.

\subsection{Why the Agentic Modes Fail on HotpotQA}

The four agentic architectures follow their source papers closely, and yet they
are worse than a plain pass on the benchmark those papers use.
The explanation is not that the implementations are wrong --- Iterative Multi-hop
achieves the highest context recall of any mode (0.986), which is exactly what a
working chain-of-retrieval should do --- but that HotpotQA does not reward what
they add.

Each of these pipelines routes the retrieved evidence through one or more
intermediate LLM calls that summarise, extract, or argue before the final answer
is written.
Every such call is a paraphrase, and every paraphrase is a chance for a detail to
drift away from the source text.
When the question needs one hop and a five-word answer, those chances accumulate
into pure loss.
When the question needs four drug profiles reconciled into a mechanism, the same
intermediate structure is what makes the answer possible at all.
The architecture did not change between those two cases; the ratio of useful
reasoning to gratuitous paraphrase did.

\subsection{When to Use Which Architecture}
\label{subsec:when_rarerag}

The evaluation points to a clear practical recommendation --- though not the one
this thesis originally expected to give. The work set out to show that \rarerag{}
was the architecture to choose; the data instead recommends simpler modes for easy
corpora and lightweight query transformation for hard ones, and advises against
deploying \rarerag{} as built. That recommendation is stated plainly below.

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{For an easy, well-indexed corpus, use Corrective RAG or Vanilla.}
    Corrective RAG gains 3.4 points of faithfulness over Vanilla on HotpotQA
    ($p < 0.001$) at 11.8\,s, loses nothing on correctness, and is the only mode
    that is never significantly worse than Vanilla on anything, on either
    benchmark.
    It is the safest default in this study.
  \item \textbf{For hard domain questions, use Multi-Agent or Query Rewriting.}
    Both gain roughly 18 points of faithfulness on DDI L4--L5.
    Query Rewriting is the better buy: it achieves a comparable gain with one
    extra LLM call rather than a planner, several executors and a synthesiser.
    That a technique this simple matches a full agentic pipeline is itself a
    result worth stating.
  \item \textbf{Avoid MADAM-RAG unless hedging is acceptable.}
    It is a grounding-for-decisiveness trade, and on a benchmark with single
    correct answers it is a bad one.
  \item \textbf{Do not deploy \rarerag{} as evaluated.}
    Its set-wise pruning starves the generator on precisely the polypharmacy
    questions the system exists to answer.
\end{itemize}

The broader point is that the router at the heart of \rarerag{} is the part of the
design the evidence actually supports, even though the pipeline around it is
not.
If the benefit of complexity depends this strongly on question difficulty, then
choosing the pipeline per query --- rather than per deployment --- is the right
structure.
The router is sound; what it routes to needs work.

\subsection{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations_eval}

\emph{The DDI benchmark is small.}
With 100 questions in total and 20 per difficulty level, confidence intervals on
the per-level cells are roughly $\pm 0.04$ wide.
The significant results on the pooled hard subset ($n = 40$) are real, but they
are the floor of what could be established, not a precise measurement.
Confirming the difficulty-scaling effect properly needs 300--500 questions; the
cost is linear and modest (about \$2 per 100 questions per mode), and this is the
single most valuable extension of the present work.

\emph{Gold answers are model-generated, then human-reviewed.}
DDI questions and reference answers were produced by gpt-5-nano from OpenFDA and
DrugBank passages and then manually reviewed against those passages for factual
consistency --- but by the author, not by a pharmacist.
They are a systematic signal, not a clinically validated one, and the correctness
figures should be read with that in mind.

\emph{One generator, one judge, one corpus.}
Every result here is conditioned on a small generator model (gpt-5-nano).
A larger generator that hallucinates less at baseline would compress the room the
elaborate architectures have to improve, and might well flip the DDI conclusion
back towards the HotpotQA one.
The claim this chapter defends is about how the benefit of complexity scales with
task difficulty for a given generator, not about an absolute ranking of
architectures.

\section{Drug Interaction Advisory Demonstration}
\label{sec:ddi_demo}

To illustrate qualitative system behaviour beyond the aggregate metrics, this
section presents a representative interaction from the \medrag{} advisory
interface using the OpenFDA drug interaction corpus.

\paragraph{A cited response.}
A synthetic patient query was submitted: \textit{``My doctor prescribed warfarin
for atrial fibrillation and my orthopaedic surgeon wants to add ibuprofen for
knee pain. Is this safe?''}
The system retrieved three passages from the OpenFDA DDI corpus covering
warfarin--NSAID interactions, identified the bleeding-risk mechanism
(inhibition of platelet aggregation combined with anticoagulation),
and generated the following response excerpt:

\begin{quote}\small
\textit{Concurrent use of warfarin and ibuprofen carries a significantly elevated
risk of serious bleeding, including gastrointestinal haemorrhage
[Source: FDA label warfarin sodium, interaction with NSAIDs].
This interaction is pharmacodynamic: ibuprofen inhibits platelet aggregation
and can irritate the gastric mucosa while warfarin suppresses clotting factor
synthesis, and the two effects are additive.
The combination should be avoided unless both prescribing physicians explicitly
accept the haemorrhage risk and agree on close INR monitoring.}
\end{quote}

Every claim in this response is traceable to a retrieved FDA passage, and the
RAGAS faithfulness score for this query was 1.0.
This is the behaviour the system is designed to produce, and the reason
faithfulness was chosen as the primary quality metric: a pharmacist reading this
answer can open the cited label and check it.

\paragraph{On abstention.}
The system also implements an abstention path, in which the grounding verifier
declines to answer when the support score falls below the threshold.
It did not fire once across either benchmark --- 0 abstentions in 1{,}000 HotpotQA
queries and 0 in 100 DDI queries.
The reason is structural rather than accidental, and it is analysed in
\cref{subsec:abstention_reality}: every question in both benchmarks is answerable
from the indexed corpus, and a low-scoring answer triggers a retry through the
self-reflection pipeline that, on these corpora, always cleared the threshold.
Abstention is therefore best understood as a last-resort safeguard for
out-of-corpus queries, which these benchmarks do not contain, rather than as an
actively exercised behaviour.
