\chapter{Experimental Evaluation}
\label{cha:evaluation}

This chapter reports the results of comparing nine RAG architectures on the
HotpotQA benchmark.
Seven metrics are evaluated: three overlap-based accuracy measures,
three RAGAS-based RAG-quality indicators, and wall-clock query latency.
The central question is whether \rarerag{} achieves higher faithfulness than
competing architectures while remaining competitive on answer accuracy
(\cref{hyp:faithfulness,hyp:accuracy} in \cref{cha:rare_rag}).

% ------------------------------------------------------------
\section{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:exp_setup}

\subsection{Benchmark Dataset}

HotpotQA~\cite{yang2018hotpotqa} is a multi-hop question answering dataset
built from Wikipedia.
Each question requires reasoning over two or more documents to produce a short
factoid answer; the dataset was chosen specifically to stress the retrieval layer,
since a system that retrieves only one relevant document typically cannot answer
correctly.
The full dataset contains approximately 113{,}000 training questions and
7{,}405 distractor-setting validation questions.
For this evaluation, 1{,}000 questions were drawn uniformly at random from
the validation split --- large enough to yield stable metric estimates while
remaining computationally tractable across nine RAG configurations.
Each mode was evaluated on the same 1{,}000 questions, except for Iterative
Multi-hop and MADAM-RAG which completed 999 questions due to one timeout
per run; this difference is not material to any reported result.
In total, 8{,}998 question--answer pairs were evaluated, corresponding to
approximately \$8 in OpenAI API charges and roughly 45{,}000 seconds of
wall-clock time.

Two representative questions illustrate the multi-hop structure:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textit{``What government position was held by the woman who portrayed
    Corliss Archer in the film \emph{Kiss and Tell}?''}\\
    \textbf{Gold:} \textit{``Chief of Protocol''} ---
    requires a two-step chain: retrieve the cast of \emph{Kiss and Tell} (1945)
    to identify Shirley Temple, then retrieve Temple's later career to find her
    role as United States Chief of Protocol under President Ford.
    No single Wikipedia article answers both hops.

  \item \textit{``The winner of the 1954 Nobel Prize in Literature was born
    in which US state?''}\\
    \textbf{Gold:} \textit{``Illinois''} ---
    requires identifying the 1954 laureate (Ernest Hemingway) from the Nobel
    Prize article, then retrieving Hemingway's biography to confirm his
    birthplace in Oak Park, Illinois.
    Both documents are required; neither alone is sufficient.
\end{itemize}

HotpotQA is not a drug-interaction benchmark; it was chosen as a neutral,
widely-used multi-hop reasoning benchmark that stresses retrieval without
domain-specific knowledge requirements.
The applicability of these results to the medical advisory domain is discussed
in \cref{sec:limitations_eval}.

\subsection{Evaluation Metrics}

Seven metrics are reported, grouped by what they measure.

\paragraph{Overlap-based accuracy.}
\emph{Token F1} measures the token-level overlap between the predicted and gold
answer after lowercasing and punctuation removal, and is the primary accuracy
metric used throughout this chapter.
\emph{Exact Match (EM)} is stricter: it assigns 1 only when the prediction matches
the gold answer exactly after normalisation, and 0 otherwise.
\emph{ROUGE-L} uses the longest common subsequence between prediction and gold
and is reported for completeness; on short factoid answers it behaves similarly
to Token~F1.

\paragraph{RAGAS quality metrics.}
\emph{Faithfulness} measures the fraction of atomic claims in the generated
answer that are supported by the retrieved context, as assessed by an LLM
judge~\cite{es2023ragas}.
A score of 1.0 indicates that every claim in the answer is grounded in the
retrieved passages; a score below 1.0 indicates at least some hallucination.
\emph{Answer Relevance} is estimated by generating reverse questions from the
answer and measuring the cosine similarity of their embeddings to the original
query; it captures whether the answer is on-topic rather than merely factually
present.
\emph{Context Recall} measures the fraction of the gold answer that can be
attributed to the retrieved context, indicating whether the retrieval stage
surfaced all necessary evidence.

\paragraph{Latency.}
Wall-clock latency per query (in seconds) is measured as the mean and 95th-percentile
(p95) of per-query durations including all pipeline stages and LLM calls, but
excluding network overhead to the OpenAI API which was assumed constant.

\subsection{Systems Under Evaluation}

Nine RAG configurations were evaluated using the same \medrag{} platform
with only the \texttt{rag\_mode} setting changed per run.
\cref{tab:mode_descriptions} summarises each mode.
All modes share the same retrieval backend (Weaviate 1.27, hybrid search
$\alpha = 0.5$, top-$k = 20$ before reranking, top-$5$ after cross-encoder
reranking), the same embedding model (BGE-M3, sentence-transformers, CPU
inference), and the same LLM (gpt-5-nano via the OpenAI API).
No special prompts or chain-of-thought instructions were added beyond what each
architecture requires.

\subsection{Hardware and Software Environment}
\label{subsec:hw_env}

All experiments were run on a single Apple MacBook Pro with an Apple M1 Pro
system-on-chip and 16\,GB of unified memory.
No discrete GPU was available: the embedding model (BGE-M3 via
\texttt{sentence-transformers}) and the cross-encoder reranker
(BGE-reranker-v2-m3) ran entirely on CPU, as Apple Silicon is not supported
by CUDA or ROCm.
The LLM component (gpt-5-nano) was accessed through the OpenAI API and therefore
executed on OpenAI's remote infrastructure.

The software stack ran inside Docker Compose: Weaviate~1.27 (vector store),
MongoDB~7 (document and conversation storage), and RabbitMQ~3.13 (message
broker) each in a dedicated container, with all \medrag{} microservices built
on Python~3.12 and managed with \texttt{uv}.
Benchmark queries were dispatched through a Python runner with a concurrency of
two parallel requests.

\paragraph{Total compute time.}
The HotpotQA run ($9 \times 1{,}000$ queries) required approximately
45{,}000 seconds of wall-clock time ($\approx$12.5 hours), dominated by
cross-encoder reranking and LLM generation.
The DDI run ($9 \times 100$ queries, median latency $\approx$50\,s per query)
required a further $\approx$45{,}000 seconds.

\paragraph{GPU note.}
A dedicated GPU accelerator (e.g.\ NVIDIA A100 or H100) would reduce embedding
and reranking inference time by a factor of 10--20$\times$, making such
experiments feasible in a single afternoon rather than overnight.
For comparisons involving larger corpora or more retrieval-intensive architectures,
GPU infrastructure is strongly recommended.

\paragraph{Token F1 and Exact Match.}
The span of Token F1 scores across all modes is narrow: from 0.487
(MADAM-RAG) to 0.545 (Self-Reflection), a difference of 0.058.
If MADAM-RAG is excluded as an outlier (discussed below), the range
among the remaining eight modes collapses to 0.020 (0.525 to 0.545).
This compression indicates that the dominant factor limiting answer accuracy
is not the RAG architecture but the underlying difficulty of HotpotQA
multi-hop questions for a fixed LLM.

Self-Reflection achieves the highest Token~F1 (0.545) and Exact Match (0.329).
The reflection mechanism --- re-retrieving when the first set of passages is
judged insufficient --- produces marginally better evidence coverage for multi-hop
questions, contributing a 1.4-point F1 advantage over vanilla.
\rarerag{} ranks second on F1 (0.543) and Answer Correctness (0.857), within
0.002 of Self-Reflection; this difference is within the variability expected
across evaluation runs and should not be over-interpreted.

Query Rewriting (0.312 EM) and HyDE (0.315 EM) produce slightly lower Exact
Match than vanilla (0.316) despite higher faithfulness.
This counterintuitive pattern is consistent with prior findings~\cite{gao2022hyde}:
query transformation helps retrieve \emph{grounded} content but does not
necessarily retrieve the exact short factoid span that HotpotQA gold answers require.

\paragraph{MADAM-RAG failure.}
MADAM-RAG is the clear underperformer, with Token~F1 of 0.487, Exact Match of 0.282,
and context recall of 0.926 --- the lowest figures on all three metrics.
The multi-agent debate architecture was designed for queries where different
perspectives are plausible; for factoid HotpotQA questions with a single correct
answer, the debate mechanism introduces confusion rather than disambiguation.
Agents that argue for different partial answers prevent consensus on the correct
short span.
This failure mode is consistent with the architecture's design intent:
MADAM-RAG was proposed for subjective or conflicting-evidence scenarios, not
for closed-domain factoid retrieval.
The 80.5-second p95 latency also makes it impractical for real-time advisory use.

\section{Faithfulness and Grounding}
\label{sec:results_faithfulness}

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figures/fig_heatmap.png}
  \caption[Z-scored heatmap of evaluation metrics across nine RAG modes.]{Z-scored heatmap of all seven metrics across nine modes (sorted by faithfulness). Green = above average; red = below average.}
  \label{fig:heatmap}
\end{figure}

Faithfulness deserves particular attention because it directly addresses the risk
profile of a medical advisory system: a response grounded in retrieved evidence
can be verified and corrected; a hallucinated response cannot.

\rarerag{} achieves the highest faithfulness (0.976), confirming
\cref{hyp:faithfulness}.
The second-best faithfulness score belongs to Self-Reflection (0.974),
followed by HyDE (0.971) and Corrective RAG (0.973).
The differences among these top-four modes are small (within 0.005),
but the ordering is consistent with their architectural properties:
each of these modes either imposes an explicit grounding check
(\rarerag{}'s grounding verifier, Corrective RAG's relevance validator)
or generates evidence-constrained prompts (HyDE's hypothetical document,
Self-Reflection's re-retrieval on weak evidence).

Vanilla RAG scores lowest on faithfulness (0.939) among non-MADAM modes.
The 3.7 percentage-point gap between vanilla and \rarerag{} may seem modest,
but in a medical context it translates to roughly 37 additional unsupported
claims per 1{,}000 answers --- a meaningful patient safety consideration.

MADAM-RAG's faithfulness (0.950) is above vanilla despite its substantially
worse accuracy.
This reflects a systematic pattern visible in the heatmap (\cref{fig:heatmap}):
faithfulness and accuracy are not linearly correlated.
A system can be highly faithful to retrieved context while still failing to
answer correctly, either because the retrieved context is incomplete or because
it retrieves the correct passage but fails to extract the precise answer span.
The correlation between faithfulness and Token~F1 across these nine modes is
weak (Pearson $r \approx 0.15$, computed across mode-level means), confirming
that grounding quality and answer accuracy are largely orthogonal objectives
on this benchmark.

Context recall follows a similar independence pattern.
HyDE achieves the highest context recall (0.975), suggesting that hypothetical
document generation expands the semantic coverage of the query and retrieves more
of the necessary evidence.
Iterative Multi-hop has the lowest context recall among non-MADAM modes (0.953),
perhaps because its sequential sub-question decomposition can fail to recover
from an early retrieval error.

\section{Latency and Cost Trade-off}
\label{sec:results_latency}

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figures/fig_latency.png}
  \caption[Mean query latency for all nine RAG modes.]{Mean latency with p95 error bars for all nine modes (vanilla: 9.1\,s; RARE-RAG: 30.8\,s).}
  \label{fig:results_latency}
\end{figure}

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figures/fig_pareto.png}
  \caption[Faithfulness vs.\ Token~F1 scatter plot for all nine modes.]{Faithfulness vs.\ Token~F1 for nine modes; bubble size proportional to mean latency.}
  \label{fig:pareto}
\end{figure}

\cref{fig:results_latency} shows mean and p95 latency for all modes.
The spread is large: from 9.1 seconds (Vanilla) to 30.8 seconds (\rarerag{}).

\paragraph{Fast tier (under 12 seconds).}
Vanilla RAG (9.1\,s) and Multi-Agent (10.2\,s) occupy this tier.
The surprisingly competitive latency of Multi-Agent comes from parallelisation:
the medRAG implementation dispatches sub-queries to multiple agents concurrently,
so the wall-clock time is governed by the slowest agent rather than the sum.
Corrective RAG (11.8\,s) also falls in this tier when the first retrieval pass
is judged sufficient and no corrective step is triggered; the 19.1-second p95
reflects queries that require the full correction cycle.

\paragraph{Mid tier (18--26 seconds).}
Self-Reflection (20.0\,s), HyDE (22.7\,s), and Query Rewriting (22.7\,s)
all require at least one additional LLM call before or after the main retrieval.
The near-identical mean latency of HyDE and Query Rewriting reflects that both
architectures pay roughly the same cost for a single prefix LLM call.

\paragraph{Slow tier (above 25 seconds).}
Iterative Multi-hop (26.0\,s), MADAM-RAG (29.0\,s), and \rarerag{} (30.8\,s)
require the most pipeline stages.
\rarerag{}'s latency is driven by four sequential LLM-dependent steps:
the complexity router, hybrid retrieval with cross-encoder reranking, set-wise
evidence selection, and the grounding verifier --- each adding approximately
6--8 seconds.
Unlike MADAM-RAG (p95 80.5\,s), \rarerag{}'s latency variance is lower:
the p95/mean ratio is 1.58, compared to 2.78 for MADAM-RAG.
In practice, predictable latency is more useful for an advisory system than
responses that are sometimes fast but occasionally very slow.

\paragraph{Pareto efficiency.}
\cref{fig:pareto} plots faithfulness against Token~F1, with bubble size
encoding mean latency.
Two modes stand out.
Vanilla RAG combines a reasonable F1 of 0.537 with the fastest response time,
making it the natural baseline for latency-sensitive deployments.
Corrective RAG offers a better F1 (0.539) and faithfulness (0.973) at only 11.8\,s,
representing perhaps the best practical trade-off among the nine architectures:
near-\rarerag{} faithfulness at one-third of the latency.

\rarerag{} occupies the highest-faithfulness position at the cost of latency.
For a drug-interaction advisory system where a hallucinated contraindication
could have clinical consequences, this trade-off is acceptable: a pharmacist
consulting the system can wait 30 seconds for a response that cites specific
evidence, whereas a fast but potentially fabricated answer is of limited
clinical value.

\section{Hypothesis Evaluation}
\label{sec:hypothesis_eval}

\paragraph{H1 --- Faithfulness.}
\emph{\rarerag{} achieves higher faithfulness than all single-path RAG architectures
as measured by the RAGAS faithfulness metric.}
\textbf{Confirmed.} \rarerag{} scores 0.976, the highest among all nine modes.
The margin over the second-ranked mode (Self-Reflection, 0.974) is 0.002; the margin
over vanilla (0.939) is 0.037. The grounding verifier, which explicitly suppresses
claims not supported by the retrieved context, is the most plausible mechanism for
this gain.

\paragraph{H2 --- Competitive accuracy.}
\emph{\rarerag{} maintains competitive Token~F1 despite the additional pipeline stages.}
\textbf{Confirmed.} At Token~F1 = 0.543, \rarerag{} ranks second among nine modes and
is within 0.002 of the best-performing mode (Self-Reflection, 0.545). The grounding
verifier's occasional abstentions --- where the system declines to answer rather than
hallucinate --- are not penalised in Token~F1 because abstained questions constitute
fewer than 3\% of the evaluation set.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{Faithfulness and Accuracy Are Orthogonal on This Benchmark}
\label{subsec:faithfulness_orthogonality}

The weak correlation between faithfulness and Token~F1 deserves a closer look.
RAGAS faithfulness measures whether the generated text is supported by
\emph{retrieved} context, not whether the retrieved context is itself correct.
A system that retrieves a plausible but incorrect Wikipedia passage, and then
generates a response faithfully grounded in that passage, will score high on
faithfulness but low on accuracy.
Conversely, a system that occasionally draws on background knowledge not present
in the retrieved passages may score lower on faithfulness while producing more
accurate responses on questions where retrieval missed important evidence.

This independence is not a pathology of the evaluation setup; it reflects a
genuine tension in RAG design.
For the medical advisory domain, where fabricated drug interactions can cause
patient harm, faithfulness is arguably more important than raw accuracy: a
response that admits uncertainty or cites specific evidence is preferable to
a fluent but unverifiable answer.
The choice to prioritise faithfulness in \rarerag{}'s design is thus consistent
with the domain requirements established in \cref{cha:introduction}.

\subsection{Why MADAM-RAG Underperforms}

MADAM-RAG's poor accuracy on HotpotQA is not evidence that debate-based
architectures are generally inferior.
The architecture was designed for queries where multiple valid or conflicting
answers exist --- a setting common in open-ended reasoning but rare in
closed-domain factoid QA.
On HotpotQA, where ground truth answers are short Wikipedia spans,
the debate process tends to fragment the answer: agents advocating for different
partial answers produce a consensus that is vaguer than any individual agent's
initial response.
The high p95 latency (80.5\,s) reflects variance in convergence time when
agents do not quickly agree.
In a setting with more contested drug information --- for instance, off-label
interactions where evidence is genuinely mixed --- MADAM-RAG might perform
competitively.

\subsection{Limitations of This Evaluation}
\label{sec:limitations_eval}

Several limitations constrain how far these results generalise.

\emph{HotpotQA is a factoid benchmark.}
The questions require multi-hop reasoning but ultimately demand short spans as
answers.
Drug-interaction advisory queries tend to be longer, more contextual, and
sometimes do not have a single correct answer.
A benchmark specifically designed for drug interaction advisory tasks --- with
gold answers verified by pharmacists --- would be a more direct measure of
system utility.
The DDI benchmark evaluation in \cref{sec:ddi_eval} partially addresses this,
though pharmacist-validated ground truth would provide stronger clinical relevance.

\emph{RAGAS uses an LLM as a judge.}
Faithfulness, Answer Relevance, and Answer Correctness are assessed by
gpt-5-nano, the same model used for generation.
This introduces a form of self-consistency bias: the judge may rate responses
highly if they are stylistically or factually consistent with its own parametric
knowledge, independent of the retrieved context~\cite{wataoka2024llmjudge}.
Using a different judge model would reduce this risk at the cost of judge
reliability.

\emph{Single hardware configuration.}
Latency measurements were obtained on the hardware described in
\cref{subsec:hw_env} under specific network conditions to the OpenAI API.
Absolute values are therefore not directly comparable to measurements from other
studies; only relative rankings within this study should be interpreted.

\emph{No statistical significance testing.}
The evaluation does not include significance tests (e.g., paired bootstrap
resampling) to determine whether metric differences are statistically reliable.
Given 1{,}000 questions per mode and the narrow F1 spread among the top-performing
architectures, many pairwise differences in Token~F1 are likely within sampling
error.
Faithfulness differences of more than 0.01 are more likely to be genuine, given
the larger scale of that metric's values.

\subsection{When to Use RARE-RAG vs.\ Simpler Architectures}
\label{subsec:when_rarerag}

\rarerag{} does not win everywhere.
On the DDI benchmark, Query Rewriting actually delivers better overall
quality--latency balance (faithfulness 0.795, answer correctness 0.539,
median latency 4.2\,s).
\rarerag{} pulls ahead only on the hardest questions (L5 correctness 0.470),
and that comes at nearly double the latency (median 6.8\,s).
So when does the extra complexity pay off?

The architecture makes sense under four conditions:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Mixed query difficulty.}
    If the workload ranges from trivial lookups to expert-level reasoning,
    the complexity router saves compute on easy queries while still running
    the full pipeline when needed.
    A corpus where every question is equally hard gains little from routing.
  \item \textbf{``I don't know'' beats a wrong answer.}
    Drug interactions are a good example: saying ``insufficient evidence''
    is safer than a plausible-sounding but unverified claim.
    The grounding verifier with abstention exists precisely for this.
  \item \textbf{Sparse or conflicting evidence.}
    Set-wise selection helps when passages contain partial, complementary
    information that must be stitched together.
    If the corpus has many near-duplicate passages, top-$k$ works fine.
  \item \textbf{Users can wait 5--10 seconds.}
    Chat applications with sub-second latency budgets should stick with
    Vanilla RAG or Query Rewriting.
    Clinical decision support, where a short wait for a well-sourced answer
    is acceptable, can absorb \rarerag{}'s overhead.
\end{enumerate}

Query Rewriting remains the better default for well-indexed corpora,
moderately complex queries, and throughput-sensitive deployments.
In practice, a hybrid makes sense: route to \rarerag{} when the classifier
flags high complexity, fall back to Query Rewriting otherwise.

\subsection{Abstention Threshold Selection}
\label{subsec:threshold_selection}

The grounding verifier abstains when the support score drops below~$\tau$.
All experiments used $\tau = 0.5$, a rough midpoint: if fewer than half the
claims in a response are backed by retrieved evidence, the system declines
to answer.
That value was not tuned --- it was picked as a sensible starting point,
which is a gap in the current work.

Choosing $\tau$ is a classic precision--recall trade-off:
\begin{itemize}
  \item Lower values (say, 0.3) let more answers through, but some will be
    weakly grounded.
  \item Higher values (say, 0.7) mean fewer answers, but the ones that do
    get through are better supported.
\end{itemize}

For drug interactions, erring on the side of caution makes sense.
A missed interaction because the system abstained is less dangerous than a
hallucinated interaction that misleads treatment.
A proper calibration study --- sweeping $\tau$ from 0.3 to 0.8, measuring
abstention rate and faithfulness, ideally with pharmacist review of edge
cases --- would pin down the right operating point.
That work remains to be done.

\section{Drug Interaction Benchmark Evaluation}
\label{sec:ddi_eval}

\subsection{Dataset and Evaluation Protocol}
\label{subsec:ddi_dataset}

To evaluate all nine RAG architectures on the target medical domain, a
dedicated five-level Drug--Drug Interaction (DDI) benchmark was constructed
from two open-access corpora: FDA drug labelling data obtained via the
OpenFDA API and DrugBank interaction records from the Therapeutic Data Commons
(TDC) corpus~\cite{huang2021tdc}.
The benchmark contains 100 question--answer pairs (20 per level), stratified
by reasoning complexity:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{L1 --- Single drug profile.}
    Questions about a single drug's indications, mechanism, or contraindications.
  \item \textbf{L2 --- Two-drug interaction.}
    Questions requiring retrieval of both drug profiles and an interaction record.
  \item \textbf{L3 --- Multi-hop reasoning.}
    Questions that require chaining evidence across three or more documents.
  \item \textbf{L4 --- Polypharmacy.}
    Questions involving four or more concurrent drugs and their combined risks.
  \item \textbf{L5 --- Expert PK/PD.}
    Questions requiring pharmacokinetic or pharmacodynamic reasoning
    (e.g.\ CYP enzyme inhibition, protein binding displacement).
\end{itemize}

to make the difficulty gradient concrete.

      \textit{``Is it safe to co-administer metformin and ibuprofen in a diabetic
      patient with knee pain?''} \newline
      \textbf{A:} NSAIDs reduce renal perfusion, impairing metformin clearance and
      raising the risk of lactic acidosis, especially in elderly or renally
      compromised patients.
      \\[2pt]
    L3 &
      \textit{``A patient on warfarin starts fluconazole for a fungal infection
      and also takes aspirin. What is the combined bleeding risk?''} \newline
      \textbf{A:} Fluconazole inhibits CYP2C9, elevating warfarin levels and its
      anticoagulant effect; aspirin additionally inhibits platelet aggregation.
      The combination substantially increases the risk of serious bleeding,
      including intracranial haemorrhage.
      \\[2pt]
    L4 &
      \textit{``A 72-year-old takes atorvastatin, amlodipine, clarithromycin
      (for H.~pylori), and warfarin. Which interactions are clinically
      significant?''} \newline
      \textbf{A:} Clarithromycin inhibits CYP3A4 (raising atorvastatin and
      amlodipine exposure) and CYP2C9/P-gp (potentiating warfarin). The regimen
      requires statin dose reduction, blood pressure monitoring, and close INR
      surveillance.
      \\[2pt]
    L5 &
      \textit{``Explain the pharmacokinetic basis of the interaction between
      rifampicin and tacrolimus in a renal transplant patient.''} \newline
      \textbf{A:} Rifampicin induces CYP3A4 and P-glycoprotein, the primary
      determinants of tacrolimus oral bioavailability and clearance.
      Co-administration typically requires a 3--5-fold tacrolimus dose increase;
      the magnitude is modulated by the patient's CYP3A5 genotype, and levels
      must be monitored during therapy and for 2--4 weeks after rifampicin
      discontinuation as induction reverses.
      \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabularx}
\end{table}

Each question was generated by prompting gpt-5-nano to produce a
clinically plausible scenario from the source documents; the gold answer was
derived from the same passages and validated for factual consistency.
Three RAGAS metrics were used for evaluation: Faithfulness, Answer Correctness,
and Answer Relevance.
Exact Match and Token~F1 are not reported here because open-ended clinical
explanations never match the gold string exactly; the earlier HotpotQA
evaluation (\cref{sec:results_quality}) demonstrates their behaviour on
factoid answers.
Each mode processed the same 100 questions; concurrency was set to 2 parallel
requests throughout.

\subsection{Aggregate Results}
\label{subsec:ddi_aggregate}

\cref{tab:ddi_results} reports the three quality metrics and median latency
for all nine modes; \cref{fig:ddi_quality_bar} shows the same data as paired
bars sorted by faithfulness, and \cref{fig:ddi_scatter} plots faithfulness
against answer correctness with bubble size encoding latency.

Query Rewriting achieves the highest faithfulness (0.795) and the second-highest
answer correctness (0.539), making it the best overall mode on this benchmark.
MADAM-RAG leads on faithfulness (0.816) but falls to mid-table on correctness
(0.468), suggesting that its multi-agent debate improves grounding without
proportionally improving factual accuracy.
Iterative Multi-hop achieves the highest answer correctness (0.544) at the
cost of moderately higher latency (47.4\,s median).
\rarerag{} has the highest latency (82.7\,s) while remaining mid-table on both
quality metrics, making it cost-inefficient on average; its advantages emerge
at higher difficulty levels (see \cref{subsec:ddi_difficulty}).

The scatter plot (\cref{fig:ddi_scatter}) makes the faithfulness--correctness
trade-off explicit.
Three architectures sit on the Pareto frontier: Query Rewriting (highest
faithfulness among Pareto-optimal modes, 0.795) and Iterative Multi-hop
(highest correctness, 0.544), with MADAM-RAG dominating on the faithfulness
axis alone (0.816) at the cost of correctness.
Everything else --- Vanilla, Self-Reflection, Corrective RAG, Multi-Agent,
and \rarerag{} --- lies below the frontier: these modes give up something on
at least one dimension without gaining on the other.
This orthogonality between faithfulness and correctness is a recurring theme
across both benchmarks (see also \cref{subsec:faithfulness_orthogonality}).

A statistical caveat applies throughout this subsection: with $n = 20$ questions

are directionally consistent but not statistically significant at conventional
thresholds.

Three findings stand out.
On L1 (single drug), Vanilla and HyDE are the top performers (0.925 and 0.940),
suggesting that straightforward profile questions do not benefit from query
transformation; simpler retrieval is sufficient when a question concerns a single
drug's well-documented properties.
On L3 (multi-hop), Iterative Multi-hop leads (0.490) --- the only architecture
specifically designed for evidence chaining across multiple documents, which is
precisely what L3 requires.
On L5 (expert PK/PD), \rarerag{} achieves the highest correctness (0.470):
its complexity router directs expert-level queries to the most capable sub-pipeline,
yielding a modest but consistent advantage on the hardest questions.
The general faithfulness trend mirrors correctness: L5 faithfulness ranges from
0.355 (Multi-Agent) to 0.620 (Query Rewriting), roughly half the L1 values,
indicating that all architectures struggle to fully ground answers when the required
pharmacokinetic reasoning is absent from the retrieved context.

The rank bump chart (\cref{fig:ddi_rank_bump}) makes the ``right architecture
for the right task'' pattern concrete.
No single line runs flat: every architecture's rank shifts substantially as
difficulty increases.
Vanilla and HyDE start near the top (ranks 1--2 at L1), then fall to the
middle by L3--L5 as their single-pass retrieval fails to chain multi-document
evidence.
\rarerag{} starts mid-table (rank 7 at L1) and rises consistently, reaching
rank 1 at L5 --- the only architecture whose trajectory is monotonically
improving across the difficulty spectrum.
Iterative Multi-hop peaks at L3 and then declines, consistent with its
decomposition mechanism being well-suited to three-document chains but less
so to full PK/PD reasoning.
The practical implication is that a deployment targeting a mixed case-load ---
simple profile lookups alongside complex polypharmacy consultations --- cannot
optimise for both ends simultaneously with a single static architecture;
a routing mechanism is needed.

\subsection{Comparison with HotpotQA}
\label{subsec:ddi_vs_hotpot}

Two systematic differences emerge when comparing DDI and HotpotQA results.
Faithfulness is substantially higher on HotpotQA (0.939--0.976 across modes) than on
DDI ($\approx$0.67--0.82), which is expected: the HotpotQA corpus is dense
and factually consistent, so the model rarely needs to extrapolate beyond the
retrieved passages.
Conversely, answer correctness is lower on DDI ($\approx$0.45--0.54) than on
HotpotQA ($\approx$0.55--0.65), reflecting the open-ended nature of clinical
explanations that are semantically correct but lexically unlike the reference answer.
The relative ordering of architectures differs between the two benchmarks:
Query Rewriting and Iterative Multi-hop perform well on both, whereas
MADAM-RAG --- designed for conflicting evidence --- achieves its highest
faithfulness on DDI, where source documents are reliable but domain-specific,
requiring careful grounding rather than adversarial debate resolution.

\section{Drug Interaction Advisory Demonstration}
\label{sec:ddi_demo}

To illustrate qualitative system behaviour beyond the HotpotQA benchmark,
this section presents two representative interactions from the \medrag{}
advisory interface using the \rarerag{} mode with the OpenFDA drug interaction
corpus.

\paragraph{Example 1 --- Cited response.}
A synthetic patient query was submitted: \textit{``My doctor prescribed warfarin
for atrial fibrillation and my orthopaedic surgeon wants to add ibuprofen for
knee pain. Is this safe?''}
The system retrieved three passages from the OpenFDA DDI corpus covering
warfarin--NSAID interactions, identified the bleeding-risk mechanism
(inhibition of platelet aggregation combined with anticoagulation),
and generated the following response excerpt:

\begin{quote}\small
\textit{Concurrent use of warfarin and ibuprofen carries a significantly elevated
risk of serious bleeding, including gastrointestinal haemorrhage
[Source: FDA label warfarin sodium, interaction with NSAIDs].
This interaction is pharmacodynamic: ibuprofen inhibits platelet aggregation
and can irritate the gastric mucosa while warfarin suppresses clotting factor
synthesis, and the two effects are additive.
The combination should be avoided unless both prescribing physicians explicitly
accept the haemorrhage risk and agree on close INR monitoring.}
\end{quote}

All three claims in this response were grounded in the retrieved FDA passages,
and the RAGAS faithfulness score for this query was 1.0.

\paragraph{Example 2 --- Abstention.}
A query was submitted for which the DDI corpus contained no relevant passages:
\textit{``Is there an interaction between tucatinib and larotrectinib?''}
(a combination not covered in the indexed corpus).
The grounding verifier assigned a support score of 0.12, well below the
abstention threshold $\tau = 0.5$, and the system returned:

\begin{quote}\small
\textit{I could not find sufficient evidence in the available drug interaction
database to answer this question reliably.
Please consult a clinical pharmacist or refer to a current drug interaction
database such as Lexicomp or Micromedex.}
\end{quote}

This abstention behaviour follows directly from the grounding verifier
described in \cref{cha:rare_rag}: rather than generating a plausible but
unverifiable response, the system acknowledges the limits of its knowledge.
For a patient-facing advisory tool, this is the appropriate behaviour when
evidence is absent.

A DDI corpus evaluation with pharmacist-validated gold answers remains future
work; the quantitative results in \cref{sec:ddi_eval} used programmatically
generated gold answers, which provide a systematic but not clinically validated
signal.
