\chapter{\rarerag{}: A Risk-Aware Routed Evidence-Graph Architecture}
\label{cha:rare_rag}

This chapter introduces \rarerag{} (\textit{Risk-Aware Routed Evidence-Graph RAG}),
an architecture designed around the specific demands of medical advisory retrieval:
high faithfulness, the ability to decline answering when evidence is thin,
and efficient handling of queries that vary widely in complexity.
The chapter motivates the design, states testable hypotheses,
and describes each of the four components in detail.

% ------------------------------------------------------------
\section{Motivation}
\label{sec:rarerag_motivation}

The architectures surveyed in \cref{cha:background} can be grouped by
how they control retrieval.
Vanilla RAG applies a fixed retrieval strategy regardless of query content.
Self-RAG~\cite{asai2024selfrag} introduces per-token reflection tokens that
modulate retrieval during generation, but does not distinguish query complexity
before retrieval begins.
Corrective RAG~\cite{yan2024crag} validates retrieved passages and triggers
re-retrieval when relevance is low; in its original form the corrective step falls
back to a web search, though the implementation evaluated in this thesis reuses the
same corpus as the original pass (\cref{cha:evaluation}).
None of these architectures routes queries by complexity at the pipeline entry
point, and none applies a post-generation grounding check that can suppress
responses when evidence is insufficient.

For a drug-interaction advisory system, these omissions have direct clinical
consequences.
A query such as ``What is the mechanism of warfarin?'' is straightforward:
a single well-ranked passage from the drug label answers it completely,
and elaborate multi-step retrieval only adds latency.
A query such as ``What is the bleeding risk of combining warfarin, ibuprofen,
and enoxaparin in a patient with chronic kidney disease stage~3?'' is genuinely
complex: it requires evidence from at least three separate passages covering
the anticoagulant--NSAID interaction, the anticoagulant--LMWH overlap, and the
renal dose adjustment.
Those passages must be \emph{complementary} rather than redundant.
Applying the same flat-ranking strategy to both queries wastes resources on
simple ones and produces incomplete evidence for complex ones.

The second gap concerns response safety.
Standard RAG generates an answer as long as it has retrieved \emph{something};
whether the retrieved content actually supports the claims in the generated
response is never checked.
The LLM may draw on parametric knowledge beyond the retrieved passages,
produce plausible extrapolations from partial evidence, or occasionally
introduce hallucinated specifics.
In a medical context, the distinction between \emph{retrieved evidence} and
\emph{model belief} is clinically significant: evidence can be verified by
a consulting pharmacist; model belief cannot.
A confident but only partially grounded response is more dangerous than an
acknowledged uncertainty.
The appropriate behaviour when evidence is absent or insufficient is to
\emph{abstain} --- to inform the user that the system cannot reliably answer
from available data rather than deliver a plausible-sounding but unsupported claim.

\rarerag{} is built around these two observations: (i)~query complexity
should determine which retrieval stages execute, and (ii)~every generated
response should be audited against the retrieved evidence before delivery,
with abstention as an explicit outcome.

\section{Architecture Overview}
\label{sec:rarerag_overview}

\rarerag{} consists of four components arranged in a conditional pipeline,
illustrated in \cref{fig:rarerag_overview}.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.82\linewidth]{figures/rarerag_overview.png}
\caption[RARE-RAG architecture overview.]{RARE-RAG pipeline: the Complexity Router assigns each query to one of eight execution modes; the chosen pipeline runs under hybrid retrieval and set-wise selection, and the Grounding Verifier either returns the answer, retries through self-reflection, or abstains when the support score stays below threshold~$\tau$.}
\label{fig:rarerag_overview}
\end{figure}

The first component, the \textbf{Complexity Router}, makes a lightweight LLM
call that triages the incoming query and selects which of the platform's eight
execution modes should handle it.
It is not a binary fast-path switch: a single-drug lookup is routed to a plain
vanilla pass, a multi-hop mechanism question to the iterative chain, and a query
whose evidence is likely to be contradictory to the debate pipeline.
The router therefore treats the other architectures surveyed in this thesis as an
inventory to draw on, rather than competing with them.
The second component, \textbf{Hybrid Evidence Retrieval}, executes BM25 sparse
retrieval and dense vector retrieval in parallel, fuses the two result lists by
relative-score fusion, and applies a cross-encoder reranker to produce a
smaller high-precision shortlist.
The third component, \textbf{Set-wise Evidence Selection}, prunes the reranked
shortlist using a greedy complementarity-aware selection step that maximises
informational diversity of the final evidence set, rather than simply taking the
individually highest-scoring passages.
The fourth component, the \textbf{Grounding Verifier}, extracts atomic claims from
the LLM's response and checks each claim against the retrieved passages; if the
aggregate support score falls below a threshold~$\tau$, the system abstains rather
than returning an ungrounded response.

The name reflects the design intent.
\emph{Risk-Aware} refers to the abstention mechanism and the grounding check:
the architecture is explicitly designed to fail safely in the presence of
missing or contradictory evidence.
\emph{Routed} refers to the complexity router that selects the execution path.
\emph{Evidence-Graph} reflects the set-wise selection step, which treats the
evidence shortlist as a set of nodes with implicit semantic relationships ---
complementarity and redundancy --- rather than as an independently scored list.
The set-wise scoring function thus implicitly reasons over the graph structure
of the evidence, even though no explicit graph data structure is maintained.

\section{Query Complexity Router}
\label{sec:router}

\subsection{Design}

The router receives a query $q$ and returns a structured triage verdict with three
fields: a \emph{complexity} class
$c \in \{\textit{simple}, \textit{standard}, \textit{complex}, \textit{multi-hop}\}$,
a \emph{conflict risk} level $r \in \{\textit{low}, \textit{medium}, \textit{high}\}$,
and the \emph{route}: the execution mode that should handle the query.
The route is drawn from the eight modes the platform implements --- vanilla, HyDE,
query rewriting, self-reflection, corrective RAG, multi-agent, iterative multi-hop,
and MADAM-RAG --- and the pipeline for that mode is then instantiated and run.

Two fields rather than one is a deliberate choice.
Complexity alone does not determine the right pipeline, because two queries of
equal complexity can fail for different reasons: one because the evidence is
spread across several documents, another because the sources disagree.
The first calls for a retrieval chain, the second for a debate between agents that
can surface the disagreement instead of silently averaging over it.
Separating conflict risk from complexity is what lets the router distinguish them.

A rule-based classifier --- for instance, one that counts drug names or detects
conditional modifiers such as ``in a patient with'' --- would be fast but brittle:
it misclassifies paraphrased complex queries and flags simple queries that
happen to mention multiple drug names in a lookup context.
A fine-tuned transformer classifier would require labelled training data that
is expensive to obtain in the medical domain.
\rarerag{} instead uses a short structured LLM call with few-shot examples,
constrained to emit the three fields above as structured output.
The call is deliberately small --- the prompt is under 800~tokens --- keeping
classification latency below one second under typical API conditions.

\subsection{Routing Criteria}

The routing policy the prompt encodes is, in outline: single-fact lookups go to
vanilla; questions whose phrasing is a poor match for the corpus vocabulary go to
HyDE or query rewriting; questions requiring evidence chained across documents go
to iterative multi-hop; questions decomposable into independent sub-questions go
to multi-agent; and any query with high conflict risk --- sources likely to
disagree, as with off-label or contested interactions --- goes to MADAM-RAG,
regardless of its complexity class.
The router defaults to a more thorough pipeline when uncertain, trading latency
for the safety of the extra reasoning.

Whether that policy is \emph{good} is a separate question from whether routing is
the right structure, and this thesis answers only the second.
The evaluation in \cref{cha:evaluation} supports the structure --- the best mode
genuinely does depend on the difficulty of the question, which is exactly the
premise routing rests on --- while showing that the pipeline the router feeds into
needs work.
An ablation isolating the router's own contribution, by comparing it against both
a fixed pipeline and an oracle that always picks the best mode per query, is the
most valuable single experiment left undone here and is set out in
\cref{cha:conclusions}.

\subsection{Trade-offs}

The router introduces a fixed overhead of approximately 0.8\,s per query
regardless of classification outcome.
On the HotpotQA benchmark (\cref{sec:exp_setup}), where questions are
Wikipedia-style multi-hop rather than medical, the majority of queries were
classified as complex, which explains why \rarerag{}'s mean latency on that
benchmark exceeds the latency of simpler architectures.
For a drug-interaction corpus where a larger fraction of queries would be
simple lookup requests, the router should reduce average latency by routing
those directly to the fast path.
A controlled ablation comparing \rarerag{} with and without routing on a
DDI-specific query set is identified as future work (\cref{cha:conclusions}).

\section{Hybrid Evidence Retrieval}
\label{sec:rare_retrieval}
\label{sec:hybrid_retrieval}

\subsection{Motivation for Hybrid Search}

Drug-interaction queries pull retrieval in two opposing directions.
Drug names and dosage thresholds are \emph{exact strings}: ``warfarin 5\,mg'' and
``Coumadin'' are synonyms that BM25 will not associate unless the index includes
synonym expansion, but ``warfarin'' and ``wafarin'' are not --- a single character
difference denotes a different substance.
BM25 is well suited to exact-string matching because it rewards term overlap
without semantic generalisation.
Mechanism descriptions, adverse effect pathways, and clinical recommendations, on
the other hand, are expressed in paraphrased, domain-specific language that varies
across sources.
Dense vector retrieval, which maps queries and passages into a shared semantic space,
handles these paraphrased descriptions better than BM25.

A hybrid approach combining both signals is therefore more robust than either alone.
Experimental evidence on the BEIR benchmark~\cite{thakur2021beir} supports
this: on biomedical retrieval tasks, hybrid retrievers consistently outperform
pure BM25 or pure dense retrieval.

\subsection{Fusion and Reranking}

\rarerag{} executes BM25 and dense vector search in parallel and fuses the two
result lists by \emph{relative-score fusion}, the strategy Weaviate exposes as
\texttt{HybridFusion.RELATIVE\_SCORE}.
Each engine's scores are first min--max normalised to $[0, 1]$ over its own
candidate list, which removes the incommensurability of a BM25 term-frequency
score and a cosine similarity, and the normalised scores are then combined by a
weighted sum:

\begin{equation}
  \text{score}(d)
    = \alpha \cdot \widetilde{s}_\text{dense}(d)
    + (1 - \alpha) \cdot \widetilde{s}_\text{BM25}(d),
  \label{eq:fusion}
\end{equation}

where $\widetilde{s}$ denotes the normalised score and $\alpha = 0.5$ weights the
two signals equally in all experiments reported here.
Documents absent from one engine's list contribute zero on that side.

The alternative, Reciprocal Rank Fusion~\cite{cormack2009rrf}, discards the scores
and combines rank positions instead.
RRF is the more conservative choice --- being rank-based, it cannot be distorted
by an outlier score --- but it also throws away the \emph{margin} between a
strongly matching passage and a marginal one, which relative-score fusion
preserves.
Since the downstream cross-encoder reranks the fused list anyway, and $\alpha$ is
tunable per project (\cref{cha:implementation}), the score-preserving option was
kept.
The top-$K = 20$ documents from the fused list proceed to reranking.

The fused list is then re-scored by a cross-encoder reranker
(BGE-reranker-v2-m3~\cite{chen2024bge}).
Unlike the bi-encoder used for dense retrieval, a cross-encoder processes
the concatenated query--passage pair and produces a single relevance score,
capturing the full interaction between query and passage tokens.
This more accurate scoring comes at higher latency, which is why cross-encoder
reranking is applied to a small candidate set (top-20) rather than the full index.
The reranker reduces the shortlist to $k_r = 5$ passages that proceed to
set-wise selection.

The hyperparameters $\alpha = 0.5$ (BM25/dense blend weight in Weaviate's
hybrid search API), $K = 20$, and $k_r = 5$ were selected based on
preliminary experiments on a held-out development subset and fixed for
all reported evaluations.
BGE-reranker-v2-m3 was chosen because it is released under an Apache~2.0
licence, runs entirely on-premise, and consistently ranks among the
top performers on the MTEB Reranking benchmark as of the evaluation
date~\cite{chen2024bge}.

\section{Set-Wise Evidence Selection}
\label{sec:setwise}

\subsection{Limitation of Point-wise Ranking}

Cross-encoder reranking produces a list sorted by per-passage relevance.
A naive top-$m$ selection takes the $m$ individually highest-scoring passages.
This has a well-known limitation: if the top two passages address the same aspect
of the query, selecting both is redundant.

A drug-interaction query about warfarin and NSAIDs might return five passages
that all describe the bleeding mechanism from slightly different angles, while
the dose-adjustment and monitoring recommendations remain unretrieved.
The underlying problem is that relevance is measured independently per passage:
point-wise ranking ignores the informational relationship between passages.
A passage that is marginally less relevant than the top-ranked passage may
contribute substantially more information if the top-ranked passage is already
selected.

\subsection{Greedy Complementarity-Aware Selection}

\rarerag{} replaces naive top-$m$ selection with a greedy set-wise procedure.
Let $\mathcal{C} = \{d_1, \ldots, d_{k_r}\}$ be the candidate set of reranked
passages and let $\mathbf{e}_i \in \mathbb{R}^d$ be the embedding of
passage $d_i$.
The selection procedure builds a set $\mathcal{S}$ iteratively.

At each step, the passage $d^*$ maximising the following composite score is
added to $\mathcal{S}$:

\begin{equation}
  d^* = \argmax_{d_i \in \mathcal{C} \setminus \mathcal{S}}
        \Bigl[
          \lambda \cdot r(d_i)
          + (1 - \lambda) \cdot \mathrm{comp}(d_i, \mathcal{S})
        \Bigr]
  \label{eq:setwise}
\end{equation}

where $r(d_i)$ is the cross-encoder relevance score normalised to $[0,1]$,
$\lambda \in [0,1]$ is a trade-off parameter (default $\lambda = 0.5$),
and the complementarity score is:

\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{comp}(d_i, \mathcal{S})
    = 1 - \max_{d_j \in \mathcal{S}} \cos(\mathbf{e}_i, \mathbf{e}_j)
  \label{eq:comp}
\end{equation}

When $\mathcal{S}$ is empty (first iteration),
$\mathrm{comp}(d_i, \mathcal{S}) = 1$ for all candidates and selection
reduces to choosing the highest-relevance passage.
Subsequent iterations reward passages that are both relevant and semantically
different from what is already in the set.

The greedy procedure runs for $m = 3$ iterations by default, producing a
three-passage evidence set.
The design rationale was that a complete drug-interaction answer needs three
evidence types --- the pharmacological mechanism, the severity classification, and
the clinical management recommendation --- which often appear in distinct passages
of a drug information database.

That rationale holds for a \emph{two-drug} question and breaks down for anything
larger, which the evaluation in \cref{cha:evaluation} demonstrates rather than
merely suspects: a polypharmacy question about four concurrent drugs needs four
drug profiles plus their pairwise interactions, and three passages cannot carry
them however complementary they are.
Fixing $m$ independently of the query is the single clearest design error in
\rarerag{}, and \cref{subsec:rare_verdict} traces the architecture's failure on
hard clinical questions directly to it.
The parameter is left at its evaluated value here so that the description matches
the system that produced the results.

\subsection{Relationship to MMR and SET-based Approaches}

The selection criterion in~\eqref{eq:setwise} is structurally identical to the
Maximum Marginal Relevance~(MMR) criterion introduced by Carbonell and
Goldstein~\cite{carbonell1998mmr} for document summarisation.
The contribution of \rarerag{} is not a novel selection algorithm but rather the
application of complementarity-aware selection as an explicit pipeline stage after
hybrid retrieval and cross-encoder reranking.
Both parameters are left at neutral defaults --- $\lambda = 0.5$, weighting
relevance and complementarity equally, and $m = 3$ --- and neither was tuned on
held-out data.
Given how badly the fixed $m$ turns out to perform on multi-drug questions, a
sweep over $\lambda$ and $m$ is an obvious and cheap experiment, and it is
proposed in \cref{cha:conclusions}.

The greedy approximation itself is not a source of error worth worrying about:
with $k_r = 5$ reranked candidates and $m = 3$, the full set enumeration is only
$\binom{5}{3} = 10$ subsets, so the greedy and optimal solutions can differ only
marginally.
The problem is not \emph{how} the three passages are chosen but that there are
three of them.

\section{Grounding Verifier with Abstention}
\label{sec:grounding_verifier}

\subsection{Motivation}

Once the LLM generates a response conditioned on the selected evidence,
there is no guarantee that every claim in the response is actually supported
by that evidence.
The model may fill gaps from parametric knowledge or extrapolate beyond what the
passages say --- and, as argued in \cref{sec:rarerag_motivation}, a claim that
cannot be traced to a retrieved passage cannot be verified by a consulting
pharmacist.

The grounding verifier addresses this by treating the retrieved passages as the
authoritative source of truth for the generated response and auditing each
claim before delivery.

\subsection{Claim Extraction and Verification}

The verifier operates in two steps.

\paragraph{Step 1 --- Claim extraction.}
A structured LLM prompt decomposes the generated response into a list of
atomic claims.
An atomic claim is a single proposition that can be independently evaluated
as supported or unsupported; compound sentences are split.
For example, the sentence \textit{``Warfarin inhibits vitamin~K epoxide
reductase, which is required for the synthesis of clotting factors~II, VII,
IX, and~X''} is split into two claims:
\textit{``Warfarin inhibits vitamin~K epoxide reductase''} and
\textit{``Vitamin~K epoxide reductase is required for the synthesis of clotting
factors~II, VII, IX, and~X''}.

\paragraph{Step 2 --- Per-claim grounding check.}
For each extracted claim $c_i$, the verifier asks whether any passage in
$\mathcal{S}$ contains sufficient evidence to support $c_i$.
This is implemented as an LLM call with binary output
(\textit{supported}\,/\,\textit{not supported}).
The aggregate faithfulness score is:

\begin{equation}
  F = \frac{|\{c_i : c_i \text{ is supported by } \mathcal{S}\}|}
           {|\mathcal{C}_\text{claims}|}
  \label{eq:faithfulness_score}
\end{equation}

This is equivalent to the RAGAS faithfulness metric~\cite{es2023ragas} applied
online as a quality gate.

\subsection{Retry, Then Abstention}
\label{subsec:abstention_reality}

The grounding score $F$ gates a three-way decision rather than a two-way one.

If $F \geq \tau$, the response is returned to the user together with citations
to the passages that supported each verified claim.
If $F < \tau$, the system does \emph{not} abstain immediately: it first retries the
query through the self-reflection pipeline, which re-drafts the answer after
scoring the sufficiency of its own evidence.
The retried answer is verified again, and only if it \emph{also} scores below
$\tau$ does the system abstain:

\begin{quote}
  \small\itshape
  ``I could not find sufficient evidence in the available database
  to answer this question reliably.
  Please consult a clinical pharmacist or refer to a current drug
  interaction database.''
\end{quote}

The threshold is $\tau = 0.3$: at least a third of the extracted claims must be
grounded in retrieved passages for a response to be returned.
A lower threshold ($\tau \to 0$) approaches vanilla RAG behaviour (always
respond); $\tau = 1$ would require every claim to be grounded, which in practice
rejects any response that includes background pharmacological context not covered
by the indexed corpus.
The value is deliberately permissive because it does not act alone --- it gates a
retry, not a refusal, and a claim-level score below 0.3 after a second attempt is
strong evidence that the corpus simply does not contain the answer.

\paragraph{The abstention path never executed.}
Across both benchmarks reported in \cref{cha:evaluation} --- 1{,}000 HotpotQA
queries and 100 DDI queries --- the system abstained \emph{zero} times.
The reason is straightforward: every question in both benchmarks was answerable
from the indexed corpus.
HotpotQA questions were drawn from the same Wikipedia dump that was indexed, and
the DDI questions were generated from the OpenFDA and DrugBank passages that were
also indexed, so the relevant evidence was always present to be retrieved.
Abstention is designed to fire on a different situation --- a question whose
answer is genuinely absent from the corpus, such as an interaction between two
drugs that were never indexed --- and neither benchmark contains such questions.
Individual first-pass answers did dip below $\tau$ (per-claim scores ranged from
0.20 to 1.00), but in every case the self-reflection retry lifted the second
attempt back over the threshold.
Abstention is therefore best understood as a last-resort safeguard for
out-of-corpus queries; measuring how well it actually works requires a benchmark
that deliberately includes such queries, which is proposed in
\cref{cha:conclusions}.

\section{Expected Properties and Hypotheses}
\label{sec:rarerag_hypotheses}

Two properties were claimed for \rarerag{} relative to the architectures
described in \cref{cha:background}, each expressed as a falsifiable hypothesis and
tested in \cref{cha:evaluation}.
Both were stated before the final evaluation and both were ultimately rejected by
it; they are kept here as originally posed, and the way they fail is discussed in
\cref{sec:hypothesis_eval}.

\paragraph{H1 --- Faithfulness.}
The grounding verifier suppresses claims not supported by retrieved context,
directly reducing the hallucination rate captured by the RAGAS faithfulness metric.
Compared to Self-RAG (which reflects on passages during generation but
performs no post-generation audit) and vanilla RAG (no grounding control),
\rarerag{} should achieve the lowest hallucination rate.

\begin{hypothesis}[Faithfulness]
  \label{hyp:faithfulness}
  \rarerag{} achieves higher faithfulness than all single-path RAG architectures
  as measured by the RAGAS faithfulness metric on the HotpotQA benchmark.
\end{hypothesis}

\paragraph{H2 --- Competitive accuracy.}
The abstention mechanism could in principle reduce Token~F1 if the system
suppresses answerable queries.
However, the permissive threshold $\tau = 0.3$ and the complementary evidence
set from the set-wise selection stage should preserve accuracy on
well-supported questions.
The expectation is that \rarerag{} maintains Token~F1 within 0.01 of the
best-performing architecture despite the additional pipeline stages.

\begin{hypothesis}[Competitive accuracy]
  \label{hyp:accuracy}
  \rarerag{} maintains Token~F1 within 0.01 of the best single-architecture
  result, despite the additional pipeline stages and the abstention mechanism.
\end{hypothesis}

\subsection{Relationship to Prior Work}

\rarerag{} is most directly related to Self-RAG~\cite{asai2024selfrag} and
Corrective RAG~\cite{yan2024crag}.
Self-RAG also employs reflection tokens to assess retrieved passages and
generated segments, but operates token by token during generation rather than
as a post-generation audit, and requires fine-tuning the language model to emit
reflection tokens --- a training requirement that \rarerag{} avoids by
using prompting only.
Corrective RAG validates retrieved passages before generation and triggers
re-retrieval if relevance is low, but does not perform a post-generation
faithfulness check and does not reason over evidence complementarity.

Adaptive-RAG~\cite{jeong2024adaptiverag} is the closest prior work on
complexity-based routing: it classifies queries into single-step, multi-step,
and no-retrieval paths.
\rarerag{} extends this idea to the medical domain and adds the three
downstream evidence-quality components that Adaptive-RAG does not address.
